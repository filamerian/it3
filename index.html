1. What is Research?

Understanding systematic inquiry, empirical evidence, and technological problem solving.

📖 Formal Definition
Research is a systematic, controlled, empirical, and critical investigation of hypothetical propositions about presumed relations among natural or artificial phenomena.
Simple Explanation: Imagine your campus enrollment portal crashes every registration period. Instead of guessing why, you systematically gather server logs, interview students, test database throughput under load, analyze metrics, and propose an optimized architecture. That structured, evidence-based process is research.
❌ Building Software Without Research

Relying on assumptions, developer intuition, and trial-and-error. Results in unoptimized apps, security vulnerabilities, and systems that fail user requirements.

✅ Building Software With Research

Grounded in measurable data, empirical benchmarking, verified user needs, and statistical proof of efficiency and security.

Real BSIT Scenario

Problem: The campus registrar office records student attendance manually via paper logs. Faculty spend 2 hours weekly computing attendance, and students complain about lost records.

💡 Did You Know?

Google's PageRank Was Academic Research!

Google's revolutionary search engine algorithm started in 1996 as a Ph.D. research project by Larry Page and Sergey Brin at Stanford University. They hypothesized that a search engine based on mathematical graph analysis of back-links would yield better web search results than existing text-counting techniques!

2. Characteristics of Scientific Research

The six core pillars that distinguish scientific empirical investigation from casual opinion.

Scientific research must possess six distinct characteristics. Click any card below to explore its technological application:

1. Systematic

Follows a structured, sequential procedure.

IT Application: Conducting database performance testing under identical network states, hardware environments, and automated script iterations.

2. Objective

Free from personal developer bias.

IT Application: Relying strictly on execution time in milliseconds or RAM footprint rather than saying "our code feels faster".

3. Empirical

Grounded in direct observation or data.

IT Application: Collecting 1,000 automated API response samples across peak and off-peak server loads.

4. Replicable

Can be repeated with identical results.

IT Application: Publishing open repository code, Docker environment scripts, and test seed data so panel members can re-run your benchmark.

5. Ethical

Protects user privacy and data rights.

IT Application: Anonymizing user survey responses and securing user passwords according to RA 10173 (Data Privacy Act).

6. Logical

Follows valid inductive/deductive reasoning.

IT Application: Drawing conclusions directly supported by your ANOVA statistical hypothesis testing.

3. Why Research Matters in IT

Bridging theoretical computer science with real-world enterprise software reliability.

Without research, software engineering is reduced to trial and error. Research provides the framework to prove that a new algorithm is faster, a cybersecurity model is safer, or a user interface design is easier to navigate.

🎯
Industry Value

Major Tech Companies (Google, Meta, Microsoft, AWS) hire research scientists to model complex cloud systems and quantitative AI models.

Transforming operational bottlenecks into validated solutions

4. The 7-Step IT Research Process

An interactive step-by-step roadmap from initial problem formulation to final thesis defense.

Click each step in the vertical research pipeline to examine detailed activities and BSIT examples:

1. Problem Identification Click to expand
Pinpointing specific gaps, inefficiencies, security risks, or computational bottlenecks in existing systems.

BSIT Example: Detecting high packet loss in campus Wi-Fi networks during peak student hours.
2. Literature Review Click to expand
Reviewing IEEE, ACM, and Scopus peer-reviewed papers to analyze how previous researchers tackled similar challenges.

BSIT Example: Comparing 10 published papers on dynamic Wi-Fi bandwidth throttling algorithms.
3. Formulating Objectives & Hypotheses Click to expand
Stating precise measurable goals and statistical hypotheses.

BSIT Example: "To reduce average packet queue latency by 30% using fuzzy-logic load balancing."
4. Research Methodology Click to expand
Designing the experimental architecture, selection of sampling methods, metrics (ISO/IEC 25010), and statistical tests.

BSIT Example: Selecting a quasi-experimental design using Wireshark network packet sniffing.
5. Data Collection & Experimentation Click to expand
Executing automated benchmarking scripts, running load simulations, or conducting structured surveys.

BSIT Example: Logging network throughput over 14 consecutive days.
6. Data Analysis & Interpretation Click to expand
Applying statistical tools (T-Test, ANOVA) to verify if performance improvements are statistically significant.

BSIT Example: Calculating a p-value < 0.05 confirming latency reduction was caused by the proposed algorithm.
7. Conclusion & Documentation Click to expand
Writing Chapter 5 of the Capstone manuscript, documenting limitations, and publishing open-source code repositories.

6. Types of Research

Exploring basic vs applied paradigms alongside quantitative, qualitative, and mixed approaches.

Basic (Fundamental) Research

Aims to expand fundamental scientific knowledge without immediate commercial or practical application.

BSIT Example: Researching new theoretical hashing algorithms or mathematical complexity classes (P vs NP).

Applied Research

Designed to solve specific, practical real-world problems for industry or community benefit.

BSIT Example: Building an AI-assisted rice crop disease diagnostic mobile app for local farmers in Western Visayas.

Quantitative Research

Relies on numerical measurement, mathematical equations, statistical hypothesis testing, and controlled metrics.

BSIT Example: Benchmarking database query speed before and after indexing across 1,000,000 records.

Qualitative Research

Focuses on understanding human experiences, thoughts, open-ended feedback, and social phenomena.

BSIT Example: Conducting open-ended interview studies on elderly users' frustration with municipal health portals.

Mixed Methods Research

Combines quantitative numerical metrics with qualitative user interviews for comprehensive evaluation.

BSIT Example: Evaluating a learning portal using automated system response time logs (Quantitative) and focus group interviews (Qualitative).

7. Research Design Explorer

Selecting the exact empirical design framework for your Capstone investigation.

Click any card to expand comprehensive research design guidelines for your Capstone:

Descriptive Design
Click to Expand

Observes and describes parameters of a system without manipulating variables.

Purpose: To paint an accurate profile of current status or user satisfaction.

BSIT Example: Assessing the current cybersecurity awareness and password habits of 500 college students.

Statistics Used: Mean, Percentage, Standard Deviation, Frequency Distribution.

Correlational Design
Click to Expand

Measures the statistical relationship between two continuous variables.

Purpose: To determine if changes in one variable correspond with changes in another.

BSIT Example: Measuring the correlation between daily screen time and student exam performance.

Statistics Used: Pearson r, Spearman Rho, Linear Regression.

Comparative Design
Click to Expand

Compares two or more distinct existing groups on a specific metric.

Purpose: To identify differences between existing categories.

BSIT Example: Comparing CPU usage of MySQL vs PostgreSQL when executing complex JOIN queries.

Statistics Used: Independent T-Test, One-Way ANOVA.

Experimental Design
Click to Expand

Manipulates an independent variable under strictly controlled laboratory conditions.

Purpose: To prove true cause and effect relationships.

BSIT Example: Testing encryption speed by randomly assigning data packets to AES-256 vs ChaCha20 on identical IoT hardware.

Statistics Used: Paired T-Test, Factorial ANOVA.

Quasi-Experimental
Click to Expand

Tests cause and effect in real-world settings where random assignment is impossible.

Purpose: Applied experiments in live educational or corporate environments.

BSIT Example: Implementing an AI tutoring system in Class A while Class B continues traditional learning.

Statistics Used: ANCOVA, Pre-test Post-test T-Test.

8. Quantitative vs Qualitative

Side-by-side comparative analysis of empirical paradigms.

Quantitative Numbers & Math
  • Focuses on hypothesis testing and measurement
  • Objective numerical data (latency, memory, scores)
  • Large sample sizes for statistical power
  • Analyzed using statistical software (SPSS, R, Python)
  • BSIT Metric: API execution latency in milliseconds
Qualitative Words & Meanings
  • Focuses on exploring concepts and experiences
  • Subjective text, transcriptions, and observations
  • Smaller, focused participant groups
  • Analyzed via thematic analysis and coding
  • BSIT Metric: UX interview statements on portal confusion
💡 Did You Know?

Ada Lovelace Wrote the First Algorithm in 1843!

English mathematician Ada Lovelace published the first algorithm intended to be carried out by Charles Babbage's mechanical computer, the Analytical Engine. Because of this seminal research paper, she is widely recognized as the world's very first computer programmer!

9. Research Ethics Checklist

Ensuring integrity, user privacy compliance, and academic rigor in software research.

Click each ethical requirement to verify compliance for your IT Capstone project:

1. Data Confidentiality & Anonymity (RA 10173 Compliance)

Ensure user passwords, IP addresses, and personal identifiable information (PII) are salted/hashed and never stored in plain text.

2. Avoidance of Plagiarism & IEEE Citation

Accurately cite all open-source libraries, machine learning models, code snippets, and literature papers.

3. Informed Consent

Obtain explicit voluntary participant consent prior to recording screen sessions or gathering UX interaction data.

4. Objective Data Reporting

Never falsify or cherry-pick benchmarking metrics to make an algorithm appear artificially faster.
